🔬 Jellyfish question (from your booklet!)

The booklet uses jellyfish as a classic exam question. Evidence jellyfish are living:

  • They feed on small fish caught in tentacles (nutrition)
  • They take in oxygen and respire
  • They respond — nerve net coordinates responses
  • They reproduce — release sperm/eggs, and some clone themselves
  • They move — rhythmic pulsations of the umbrella
💡 Technique: these questions want evidence FROM the passage + linking it to the characteristic name. Quote → name → explain.

🎓 The booklet also tests Chlamydomonas — a single-celled protoctist with chloroplasts, flagella and an eyespot. It's not a plant because it's single-celled and can swim using flagella. It gets energy by photosynthesis and its flagella let it move.

🧿 Levels of organisation

organelle → cell → tissue → organ → system. Example: nucleus (organelle) → muscle cell → muscle tissue → heart (organ) → circulatory system.

🦠 Bacteria & viruses

Bacterium (prokaryote)

Bacterium: cell wall/membrane, cytoplasm, circular DNA (no nucleus!), plasmids, ribosomes, sometimes a flagellum. Prokaryote = no nucleus.

Virus (NOT living)

Virus: protein coat around one type of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA). No cells, no cytoplasm, no organelles — only reproduces inside a host cell. Not living!

🔭 The light microscope

Light microscope: eyepiece → tube → objective lenses → stage (holds slide with clips) → lamp. Coarse focus (find specimen, low power only) then fine focus (sharpen; never coarse at high power).
🎓 Magnification: Magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Total = eyepiece × objective (e.g. 10 × 40 = 400×).
⚠️ SAFETY: Never touch the eyepiece/objective glass (use lens tissue). At high power, use only the fine focus knob — using the coarse knob can crack the slide or damage the lens!

How to use a microscope — correct order

  1. Switch on the microscope (turn on the lamp).
  2. Turn the nosepiece so the low-power objective clicks into place above the stage.
  3. Place your slide on the stage and secure it with the stage clips.
  4. Use the rough/coarse focus knob to move the stage to its lowest position.
  5. Look through the eyepiece and locate the specimen using the coarse knob (adjust slide position with the stage).
  6. Once in focus, increase magnification by turning the nosepiece to a higher-power objective.
  7. Sharpen with the fine focus knob — do NOT use the coarse knob (damages lens / cracks slide).

🌊 Lessons 3–6 — Diffusion, Osmosis & Active Transport

🎓 This is the highest-mark topic in Unit 1. You MUST be able to compare all three processes in a table (2.15) and explain the factors that affect rate (2.16).

💨 Lesson 3 — Diffusion

Diffusion = net movement of particles from high → low concentration (down the gradient), until evenly spread.
📌 Definition (memorise): Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration (down the concentration gradient). It is a passive process — no energy needed.
What can diffuse in/out of cells?
  • Into: oxygen, glucose, amino acids, mineral ions, water
  • Out of: carbon dioxide (waste), urea

🧊 Surface area : volume ratio — why size matters


SA:Vol = 6:1 2 cm cube
SA:Vol = 3:1 4 cm cube
SA:Vol = 1.5:1 As size ↑, SA:Vol ratio ↓ — materials take longer to reach the centre
SA:Vol = surface area ÷ volume. Small cube: 6 ÷ 1 = 6. Bigger cube: (6×4²)÷(4³) = 96÷64 = 1.5.

The agar cube practical from your booklet: cubes of agar soaked in acid lose their pink colour as acid diffuses in. The small cubes clear fastest because they have a higher SA:Vol. As cube size doubles, surface area quadruples (×2²) but volume goes up ×8 (×2³) — so the ratio shrinks.

⚠️ Exam link (2.51): "Explain why simple unicellular organisms can rely on diffusion but humans can't." → Single cells have a large SA:Vol, so diffusion across the membrane is fast enough. Large organisms have a small SA:Vol → diffusion is too slow → they need transport systems (blood, lungs, heart).

💧 Lesson 4 — Osmosis


(more water) CONCENTRATED solution
(less water) partially permeable membrane WATER diffuses through the membrane → solute molecules (orange) are too big to pass — only water moves
Osmosis: water moves from dilute → concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane. Orange = solute (too big to pass).
📌 Definition (memorise): Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a dilute solution to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane. It's diffusion of WATER, and it's passive (no energy).
⚠️ Direction of water: water always moves to the side with more solute / less water — i.e. "from where there's lots of water to where there's less".

🔴 What happens to an ANIMAL cell?

Animal cells have NO cell wall — too much water in → they swell and can burst (lyse). Too little → they shrink.

🌱 What happens to a PLANT cell?

Plant cells are protected by the cell wall. Turgid = full of water, firm. Plasmolysed = water lost, membrane pulls away from wall.
  • Turgid: dilute solution → water enters, vacuole swells, presses against cell wall → turgor pressure keeps plant firm.
  • Flaccid: isotonic → water in = water out → no turgor pressure.
  • Plasmolysis: concentrated solution → water leaves, membrane + cytoplasm pull away from cell wall → plant wilts.

🥔 Potato practical (Lesson 5) — the classic osmosis investigation

Potato cubes in different concentrations of sugar solution (0 to 1.0 mol dm⁻³).

ConcentrationWhat happensWhy
0 mol dm⁻³ (pure water)Mass increasesWater enters cells by osmosis (cell sap more concentrated than outside)
Low sugarMass increases a littleWater still enters — outside still more dilute than cell sap
≈ isotonicMass stays the sameNo net movement — same water concentration (this is the concentration of the cell sap!)

🎓 Variable names (learn these!): Independent = sugar concentration. Dependent = change in mass. Control variables = same potato size/mass, same temperature, same time, same solution volume. Use % change in mass (not raw change) so cubes with different start masses can be compared fairly.
📈 Reading the graph: as concentration ↑, % change in mass ↓. Where the line crosses 0% change = the isotonic concentration = concentration of the potato cell sap.

⚡ Lesson 6 — Active Transport

Active transport: carrier proteins use energy from ATP to pump ions/molecules against the concentration gradient (low → high).
📌 Definition (memorise): Active transport is the movement of substances from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution (against the concentration gradient). It requires energy from respiration (ATP) and uses carrier proteins in the membrane.
  • ⚡ Needs energy (ATP) from aerobic respiration in mitochondria
  • 🔁 Goes AGAINST the gradient (low → high concentration)
  • 🧬 Uses carrier proteins — specific to each substance
  • 🥔 Example: root hair cells absorbing mineral ions (e.g. nitrate) from very dilute soil water
  • 🍞 Example: small intestine epithelial cells absorbing glucose and amino acids
🎓 Why lots of mitochondria? Cells doing active transport need lots of energy → they contain many mitochondria. That's why the exam loves epithelial cells + root hair cells.
⚠️ Cyanide experiment (know this!): cyanide stops respiration → no ATP → active transport stops. In the booklet's table, glucose and galactose absorption dropped with cyanide (they need active transport), but xylose and arabinose stayed the same (diffusion only, no energy needed).

🎓 Exam phrase: diffusion and osmosis are passive (require no energy); active transport requires energy and goes against the gradient.

🦠 Lesson 7 — Pathogens & Disease

🎓 A pathogen is a microorganism that causes infectious disease. Pathogens can be fungi, bacteria, protoctists or viruses (1.4).

What each type of pathogen does

TypeWhat it isHow it harms usExample disease
BacteriaSingle-celled prokaryotesReproduce fast in the body; release toxins that damage tissueCholera, pneumonia, food poisoning (Salmonella)
FungiEukaryotes with hyphae (or single-celled like yeast)Grow on/in skin or plants; damage tissueRingworm
ProtoctistsSingle-celled eukaryotesEnter blood / cells; damage tissuesMalaria (Plasmodium)
VirusesProtein coat + DNA/RNA, no cellsInvade cells, reproduce inside them, destroy themFlu, HIV/AIDS, Tobacco Mosaic Virus

Diseases table — cause, spread, symptoms

DiseasePathogen typeHow it's spreadSymptoms
Flu (influenza)VirusAirborne droplets (coughing/sneezing)Fever, aches, fatigue
PneumoniaBacterium (Pneumococcus)Airborne dropletsInflamed lungs, difficulty breathing
RingwormFungusDirect skin contact / contaminated surfacesItchy, circular skin rash

💡 Key facts to remember

  • TMV prevents chloroplast formation → leaves lose colour → plant can't photosynthesise as well → less growth.
  • Malaria's vector is the mosquito — the pathogen Plasmodium is a protoctist (single-celled eukaryote!).
  • Bacteria harm via toxins. Salmonella = food poisoning; Lactobacillus = yoghurt (useful bacterium).
  • Viruses are not cells — they hijack host cells to reproduce.
  • The disease is not the pathogen — the pathogen causes the disease.
